\begin{abstract}
Counterexample-guided repair asks a synthesizer to fix a program given a
description of how it failed. When that synthesizer is a large language model,
the description can range from a raw execution trace to a formal certificate
carrying a violated predicate, a signed robustness value, a minimized falsifying
input, and the program location responsible. We hold the falsifier, the program,
the counterexample corpus, the token budget and the evaluation suite fixed, vary
only the rendering of the failure across five conditions, and run 300 one-step
repair attempts against an interpretable behaviour-tree policy for an unprotected
left turn in CARLA. The conditions differ sharply in what kind of repair is
attempted: structural edits account for $59/60$ attempts under a raw trace
against $13/60$ under a natural-language summary, a difference of $76.7$ points
with $95\%$ interval $[+61.7,+90.0]$. Because the raw trace also differs from
the other conditions in length and in information content, we report this as a
difference between feedback conditions, not as an effect of formal structure.
The central finding concerns the success criterion itself. Satisfying the safety
contracts on the failing scenario is trivially achievable by refusing to act: of
the 18 attempts that satisfied the contracts, 13 never entered the junction's
conflict zone; of
the other 5, 3 completed the manoeuvre, and 1 of those also left the regression
suite intact. That one program satisfies the contracts and completes the turn on
all twenty counterexamples, so the language can express a repair that passes
this corpus. Why it was found only once, whether because of search difficulty,
the baseline's structure, or a criterion that rewards inaction, remains open.
Excluding refusals, we did not detect a reliable improvement in repair success
from any condition, and under stated assumptions only large improvements would
reliably have been detected. Evaluations of verifier-in-the-loop repair should score
contract satisfaction, task completion and regression separately, with
completion scored on the same inputs as satisfaction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) is a long-established pattern in
program synthesis~\citep{solarlezama2006}. A synthesizer proposes a candidate, a
verifier tries to refute it, and each refutation constrains the next proposal.
Recent work substitutes a large language model for the synthesizer, and tests or
execution feedback for the
verifier~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine,shinn2023reflexion,chen2024selfdebug,orvalho2025cegis}.
In safety-critical control the natural verifier is not a test suite but a
falsifier: a search over scenario parameters for an input on which the policy
violates a temporal-logic specification.

That substitution raises a design question usually settled by intuition. A
falsifier can return a great deal of structure. It can report which predicate was
violated, by what signed margin, at which timestep, under which minimal
parameter assignment, at which boundary of the failing region, and which part of
the program was responsible. None of it is free: delta debugging and boundary
search cost simulations, and every field costs tokens. The intuition is that more
structure repairs better. We set out to test it (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

What we found is that the feedback conditions differ sharply in what repair is
attempted, that, excluding refusals, we could not detect any of them improving
repair success, and that pursuing the second question turned up a more useful one. Our
most transferable finding concerns how repairs come to satisfy a specification
that asks only for safety.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Contract satisfaction on a failing scenario is trivially achievable by
  refusing to act, and measuring task completion does not guard against this
  when completion is scored on a different input distribution. We separate three
  criteria (contracts satisfied, manoeuvre completed, regression suite intact)
  and show how far apart they are (\S\ref{sec:refusal}).
\item The feedback conditions differ sharply in the kind of repair attempted, a
  $76.7$-point difference in structural-edit rate between the raw-trace and
  natural-language conditions. The raw trace also differs in length and
  information content, so we do not attribute this to formal structure
  (\S\ref{sec:patchtype}).
\item A constructive test of what the language permits. Hand-written repairs
  isolate three mechanisms, two in the baseline and one in a language
  predicate, that defeat natural small edits, and the single clean repair found
  by the model shows that the
  language can express a program that resolves and completes all twenty
  counterexamples (\S\ref{sec:dsl}).
\item No detected improvement in repair success once refusals are excluded,
  with intervals that treat
  counterexamples as the independent units and a detectable-effect estimate under
  stated assumptions (\S\ref{sec:null}), and a structural precondition,
  $\Hnc > 0$, for when blame localization can carry information at all
  (\S\ref{sec:blame}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLMs that write control programs.}
Code-as-policies approaches have language models emit executable
policies~\citep{liang2023codeaspolicies}, with prompt construction following the
import-block convention of ProgPrompt~\citep{singh2023progprompt}. These
synthesize but do not falsify.

\paragraph{Adversarial scenario generation.}
Falsification and adversarial scenario search for driving policies are well
established~\citep{wang2021advsim}, with declarative scenario specification
following Scenic~\citep{fremont2019scenic}. These falsify, but where they repair
at all they do so by retraining weights or augmenting data.

\paragraph{Counterexample-guided LLM repair.}
Closest to this work, \citet{orvalho2025cegis} run a CEGIS loop with an LLM
synthesizer over introductory programming assignments, using a test suite as the
oracle and MaxSAT-based fault localization to produce a bug-free program sketch.
They find that counterexample guidance substantially improves repair across six
models and yields smaller fixes. They ask whether counterexample feedback helps at
all; we hold the counterexample fixed and vary its rendering, so our failure to
detect an improvement is not in tension with their result. Their oracle is a test
suite over discrete inputs, where ours is a robustness falsifier over a
continuous scenario space, and their programs are scored only on correctness,
whereas ours must satisfy a safety specification \emph{and} complete a task,
which is the tension \S\ref{sec:refusal} turns on.

\paragraph{LLM self-repair.}
Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine}, Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion}
and Self-Debugging~\citep{chen2024selfdebug} iterate on free-form or test-derived
feedback. Two of their results shaped this design. Removing Reflexion's
natural-language self-reflection returns pass@1 exactly to the base model's
$0.60$, against $0.68$ for full Reflexion, so every structured condition here also
carries a verbalized narrative. Self-Debugging reports that adding an execution
trace to unit-test feedback \emph{lowers} accuracy on TransCoder ($87.9\%$ against
$91.6\%$), so we did not assume more structure would help.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task, simulator, and contracts}

The task is an unprotected left turn, a permissive circular green rather than a
green arrow, at a fixed signalized four-way junction in
CARLA~\citep{dosovitskiy2017carla} Town05, recorded by exact junction
identifier. The simulator runs synchronously at \mbox{20\,Hz} with fixed physics
substepping. Sensing is ground-truth state and weather is fixed.

\paragraph{Reproducibility, stated precisely.}
The ego trajectory reproduces bit-identically under identical scenario
parameters, within a process and across restarts, and four of the five contracts
reproduce exactly in controlled repeats. The exception is $\rhoc{4}$, which varies by up to
\mbox{0.69\,m} because the lead vehicle's braking trigger reads its position back
from the simulator. $\rhoc{4}$ was the binding contract on the counterexample in
$0$ of $300$ attempts.

Conjunction as minimum (Table~\ref{tab:contracts}) gives blame localization for free, and masks every
contract except the weakest. We therefore report per-contract robustness rather
than $\rho$ alone; \S\ref{sec:regression} gives a result invisible under the
minimum.

\subsection{Program representation and baseline}

The policy is a behaviour tree in a small domain-specific language (DSL), parsed to an
AST we own and compiled to \texttt{py\_trees}. Owning the AST is what enables
tree edit distance, node-level blame, and validation before execution.
Composites compile without memory, so every predicate is re-evaluated from the
root on each tick.

The baseline is LLM-synthesized from one fixed prompt, sampled 18 times; two
responses were truncated at the $16{,}000$-token output cap then in use, and 14 of
the other 16 were valid programs. One was \emph{selected} from these against two gates measured on a
shared 100-scenario calibration set: a violation rate in $[0.20,0.40]$, and task
completion above $60\%$, the latter because a program can reach a low violation
rate simply by never entering the junction. The frozen baseline violates on
$20$ of the $100$ calibration scenarios, enters the junction in all of them, and
completes the turn in $90$. Violation rates for all 14 candidates are in the
released data.

\subsection{Falsification, certificates, and the five conditions}
\label{sec:conditions}

The falsifier samples a six-parameter scenario space uniformly, then refines
locally around low-robustness points, ranking on per-contract robustness rather
than $\rho$, because the minimum can cancel a parameter that moves $\rhoc{1}$ and
$\rhoc{2}$ in opposite directions. Plausibility constraints are enforced at
sample time following Scenic~\citep{fremont2019scenic}. Each admitted
counterexample is minimal (delta-debugged~\citep{zeller2002delta}), generalized
(each surviving parameter binary-searched to where $\rho$ crosses zero; seven of
these boundaries are withheld, \S\ref{sec:limitations}),
actionable (a violation-free response existed), and verbalized. We banked
$K{=}20$ counterexamples at a mean cost of $26.6$ simulations each.

F1 is not a subset of the other conditions (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}) in
either direction. The raw trace
carries the behaviour-tree node columns, so it contains the blame signal F2 and
F3 lack, unlabelled and unaggregated but present. It does not name the violated
contract, and carries no robustness value, scenario parameter or boundary. An
unwindowed F1 prompt measures 202{,}812 tokens at the median, $10.1\times$ the
per-attempt budget of 20{,}000, so it must be windowed. The largest window we
tested that fits every certificate ($\pm 10$ rows) cannot reach the transition
F4b names, which lies 28 to 47 rows back in all twenty cases. The labels F1 to
F4b order the conditions by how much the failure is processed, not by how much
information they carry (Table~\ref{tab:info} in Appendix~\ref{app:info}).

\paragraph{Scope, as planned and as run.}
Our internal study plan pre-specified three contracts and four scenario parameters;
we ran five and six, a change made before the baseline was frozen and explained
in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}. The pre-specified blame field is
\texttt{active\_node}. The field \texttt{authorising\_node} was defined after
observing that the first was constant; it is exploratory, and no claim in this paper rests on
F4b outperforming F3.

\paragraph{Generation settings.}
All 300 repair calls use \texttt{claude-opus-5} at effort \texttt{high}, streaming, with
\texttt{max\_tokens} $=48{,}000$ and prompt caching on the invariant system
block. The model exposes no temperature, \texttt{top\_p} or \texttt{top\_k}
parameter; \texttt{effort} is the only sampling control and is held constant.
The served model identifier is logged per call alongside the requested one, and
both were \texttt{claude-opus-5} on all 300 calls. The replicate index is not a sampling seed: replicates are
independent samples, not reproducible ones. We measured $3.00$ distinct programs
per (condition, certificate) cell out of $3$. The prompt budget $B = 20{,}000$
caps the input and is what forces F1 to be windowed; \texttt{max\_tokens} caps the
output, and no response reached it.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Five conditions, twenty certificates and three replicates give 300 repair
attempts. Each patched program is evaluated on 61 scenarios: the counterexample,
a 30-scenario regression suite the baseline passes, and a 30-scenario held-out
suite drawn from a reserved interior region the falsifier never sampled.
Outcomes fall in three buckets never pooled: \emph{truncated} (generation hit the
output cap, a harness failure), \emph{malformed} (validator rejection), and
\emph{ok}. Across all 300 attempts we observed no malformed and no truncated
outputs, so every failure is a valid program that did not fix the problem.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} All five conditions are run on the same twenty
counterexamples, three times each, so attempts are not independent and we treat
the counterexample as the unit of analysis. Every interval in the main text is a $95\%$
percentile interval from a paired cluster bootstrap: each of $10{,}000$ resamples
draws twenty counterexamples with replacement, keeps all fifteen attempts on
each, and takes differences between conditions within that resample, so two
conditions are always compared on the same cases. We apply no correction for
multiple comparisons; under a Bonferroni correction over every difference we
compute, only the structural-edit differences of F1 from each other condition
still exclude zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}).

\subsection{The feedback conditions differ in the kind of repair attempted}
\label{sec:patchtype}

Every patch is classified automatically, with no manual labelling, by comparing
the \emph{skeleton} of the patched program, the ordered tree of node names with
all parameters removed, against the baseline's. A patch that changes the
skeleton is a \emph{skeleton edit}; one that keeps it and changes only numeric
parameters, thresholds or set-points, is a \emph{hole fill}. The categories and
the classifier were fixed before the campaign (Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}), but
comparing their rates across conditions was not a recorded prediction, so we
report this contrast as an observed effect rather than a confirmed one. It is the
largest and most robust condition-dependent effect we measure.

Skeleton edits numbered 59, 13, 15, 15 and 28 of 60 under F1 to F4b
(Figure~\ref{fig:patchtype}, Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}): the model restructured the program in $98.3\%$ of
attempts under the raw trace and in $21.7\%$ under the natural-language summary,
a difference of $76.7$ points with $95\%$ interval $[+61.7,+90.0]$. F4b sits
between the others at $46.7\%$; its interval against F3, $[-3.3,+45.0]$,
includes zero.

The robust claim is narrower than ``more structure gives more control'', and
narrower than ``formal structure changes the edit''. Edit rates are not monotone
in structure: the least processed condition produces by far the most structural
edits, while F2, F3 and F4a, which add structure step by step, differ by at most
two. More importantly, F1
differs from every other condition in three ways at once. It is unprocessed, it
is roughly 3.5 times as long ($16{,}382$ against $4{,}584$ to $4{,}871$ median
prompt tokens, Table~\ref{tab:conditions}), and it carries different information
(\S\ref{sec:conditions}). What this design can establish is that the raw-trace
condition differs from every processed condition, by $51.7$ to $76.7$ points
with every interval excluding zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). It cannot
say which of the three differences is responsible. A token-matched control would not settle this
either: equalizing length leaves the information difference in place, and
truncating the trace to match would change what it contains.

\subsection{Contract satisfaction is trivially achievable by refusing to act}
\label{sec:refusal}

The success criterion pre-specified in our internal study plan is $\rho \geq 0$ on the failing scenario
after the patch. We report it as one of three criteria, each scored independently
on every attempt (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}): whether the contracts are satisfied on
the counterexample (S; we then say the patch \emph{resolves} it), whether the
manoeuvre is completed on it (C), and whether
the regression suite is left intact (R). A program that never enters the
conflict zone satisfies S for free:
$\rhoc{2}$ and $\rhoc{3}$ become vacuous, since one cannot fail to yield without
entering, and are excluded from the argmin; $\rhoc{1}$ is satisfied because no
actor is ever nearby; and $\rho$ falls through to $\rhoc{5}$, which every such
program passed.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.64\linewidth]{figures/fig4_funnel.pdf}
\caption{From 300 attempts to one clean repair: 13 of the 18 that satisfied the
contracts never entered the conflict zone. The unpatched baseline entered it on
all 20 counterexamples, so the refusals are introduced
by the patches rather than inherited.}
\label{fig:funnel}
\end{figure}

This is the synthesis-side analogue of \emph{vacuous satisfaction} in model
checking, where a specification passes for reasons unrelated to the behaviour it
was written to constrain, and where detecting such passes is a standard
obligation rather than an
afterthought~\citep{beer1997vacuity,kupferman2003vacuity}. Vacuity detection asks
whether a formula held trivially; the analogous question for repair is whether a
patch satisfied the contracts by making them inapplicable, and on this corpus 13
of the 18 that satisfied them did.

Applied in turn, the three criteria select 18, then 3, then 1 of 300 attempts
(Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}).
Thirteen of the 18 that satisfied the contracts, $72\%$, never entered the
conflict zone; two more entered but had not completed the turn when the episode
ended; three completed it, and of those three only one also left the regression
suite intact. Across 300 attempts there is exactly one clean repair.

No single criterion identifies a repair, which is why we report all three. R
is itself a contract check on other scenarios, so a program that does not drive
passes it too: one patched program completed none of the 60 suite scenarios and
caused no regression failure. C alone is no better a measure of repair, since
30 of its 33 successes are on the two counterexamples the unpatched baseline
already completed. Only the conjunction identifies a repair.

The obvious defence is to measure task completion as well, and in the form we
pre-specified it does not work. Our pre-specified completion metrics were
computed over the 60-scenario evaluation suite, while success was computed on the
single counterexample. Suite completion does catch a program that refuses
everywhere, like the one above, but the other twelve refusals completed $93$ to
$95\%$ of suite scenarios, against $95\%$ for the unpatched baseline. No
pre-specified metric measured completion on the scenario the success metric
reads from; C in Table~\ref{tab:criteria} does. Any evaluation that scores
specification satisfaction on one input distribution and task completion on
another inherits this defect.

\paragraph{A proposed remedy, untested.} One candidate fix is in the specification
rather than the feedback: require completion on the failing scenario, or run a
vacuity check in the model-checking sense and return its verdict to the model.
We have not tested either. Whether they reduce resolution by refusal, or move it
to a different degenerate answer, is future work.

\subsection{What the language permits, and why repairs may resolve by refusal}
\label{sec:dsl}

Because every attempt produced a valid program, the failures leave open whether
the language could express a correct repair at all. We tested this
constructively, writing candidate repairs by hand in the same vocabulary and
running them on all twenty counterexamples (Table~\ref{tab:dsl}).

\centering
\small

Three mechanisms defeat the natural small edits, two in the baseline and one in
the language's yield predicate, each measured rather than inferred. First, the baseline's committing branch is guarded only by a
stop-line distance test satisfied once the ego is \emph{past} the line, and it
outranks the gap-guarded branch, so the gap thresholds are unreachable and
raising them changes nothing. Second, the yield predicate is structurally blind
to a stationary obstacle: it reports time-to-arrival and, to keep the arithmetic
well defined, excludes any vehicle slower than \mbox{0.5\,m/s}, so in one failing
scenario a vehicle \mbox{9.4\,m} away at \mbox{0.04\,m/s} is discarded, the
predicate returns its 30\,s cap, and a guard demanding 15\,s of gap passes while
that car occupies the junction. Third, the geometric predicate does see it and
does block the turn, but the fallback decelerates to \mbox{1.0\,m/s} rather than
to rest, so the ego creeps across the boundary two seconds later and an unguarded
branch commits.

Adding a true stop to the geometric guard resolves all twenty counterexamples and
completes none of them. That is the degenerate repair.

\paragraph{A safe and completing repair is nonetheless expressible here.}
One attempt in 300, under the raw-trace condition F1, produced a program that
resolved its counterexample, completed the manoeuvre and caused no regression
failure. Re-evaluated across all twenty
counterexamples, including the nineteen it was never shown, \textbf{it resolves
and completes every one}. Its patch adds a 12\,m path check to the committing
branch, lengthens the two path checks at the stop line to 18\,m and brakes the
fallback harder (2.5 against 2.0\,m/s$^2$) while leaving it able to creep. Our
hand-written path checks on the same branch (16 to 32\,m, fallback unchanged)
fixed none of the twenty, so which of these differences matters is not
established. Its own comment identifies the second mechanism: a stalled vehicle
reports an effectively infinite time to arrival, so the gap test cannot see it.

What this establishes is narrow. The failures we observe are not explained by
the vocabulary being unable to state such a repair, although its yield predicate
defeats the most natural one. It
does not establish that search is the bottleneck, let alone
the only one, and a single success in 300 attempts says little about how hard
that search is. Two explanations for why most resolutions were refusals remain
open, and our data cannot separate them. The first is the starting point: the
mechanisms above defeat the natural small edits to this program in this
language, leaving a guard with a true stop as the only hand-written edit that
resolved anything; with a different branch ordering this might not hold. The second is
the criterion: a specification that rewards only safety gives refusal full
credit, so nothing in the feedback penalizes it. A second baseline would test the
program-specific part of the first, and a specification that rejects vacuous satisfaction would test the
second (\S\ref{sec:refusal}). We ran neither.


\subsection{Repair success: no detected improvement}
\label{sec:null}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/fig2_uncertainty.pdf}
\caption{Paired differences against F3 with $95\%$ intervals from a bootstrap
clustered on certificate, which respects both the shared counterexample corpus
and the three repeated attempts per cell. Only the raw-trace condition separates
from zero (an interval ending exactly at zero, where a condition has no
successes, does not), and on repair success that separation is an artefact of
refusal: excluding refusals, its interval includes zero like every other.}
\label{fig:uncertainty}
\end{figure}

After excluding refusals (the `entered' column of Table~\ref{tab:criteria}) we
did not detect a reliable improvement in repair success from any feedback
condition (Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty}). Every paired comparison against F3 has an
interval containing zero; for F2, F4a and F4b, which have no such successes,
zero is the upper endpoint. An interval containing zero is not evidence that the
effect is absent, only that this design did not detect one. The raw-trace
condition clears zero on the pre-specified rate, but 10 of its 13 resolved
attempts are refusals, and
excluding those the difference is $+1.7$ with an interval of $[-5.0,+8.3]$.

We make no significance claim about improvement in repair success, and we do
not claim a null. With $K{=}20$
counterexamples, three replicates and between zero and thirteen successes per
condition, the design supports neither. Appendix~\ref{app:mde} estimates by
simulation how large a true improvement this design could detect. Under the
assumptions stated there, an improvement over F3's $3.3\%$ is detected with
probability $0.8$ only once it reaches $13$ to $20$ percentage points, and one of
$5$ points at most a quarter of the time. These figures describe the design under
those assumptions; they are not a measured detection limit.

\subsection{Repairs were net destructive, and the minimum hides part of it}
\label{sec:regression}

Across all 300 attempts, 224 ($74.7\%$) caused at least one previously passing
regression scenario to fail, against 18 that satisfied the contracts. The modal
outcome of a repair attempt is that it did not fix the counterexample and broke
one thing that worked. This holds in every condition. Besides the F1 contrasts,
one other interval excludes zero without adjustment: F4a broke at least one
regression scenario more often than F3, by $+16.7$ points $[+3.3,+30.0]$. Among
22 unadjusted intervals about one would exclude zero by chance, and this one does
not survive the Bonferroni correction; since F4a carries no information beyond
F3, a real effect would be one of redundant text, not of blame. No condition returned regression results to
the model, so an iterated loop that does might behave differently, but as
measured here these patches should not be applied without regression checking.

Per-contract reporting reveals a second effect invisible under the minimum:
mean $\rhoc{5}$ on the counterexample falls in every condition, from $1.000$ to
between $0.59$ and $0.93$, so patches on average command harsher acceleration or
braking, and whenever $\rhoc{1}$ is the argmin this is masked completely.

\subsection{When blame localization can carry information}
\label{sec:blame}

Assigning blame to the leaf executing at the argmin is vacuous for a program like
ours, whose highest-priority branch is a region test followed by a single action,
whenever the violated contracts are evaluated over that same region, because only
that action can run there. On our baseline $H(\texttt{active\_node} \mid \text{contract}) =
0.000$ bits against $H(\text{contract}) = 1.234$ bits, and we verified by
construction that five contracts each blaming one node still give zero, so this
is a property of program structure, not of contract count. We therefore propose a
precondition, cheap to check before running such an experiment: $\Hnc > 0$.
Where it fails, the blame field re-encodes the contract label. It may still
influence the model, as redundant text can, but it carries no information beyond
the label. Defining blame instead as the last distinct leaf active before the
failing leaf began running, the decision that authorized the action rather than
the action executing when the consequence landed, raises this to $0.500$ bits;
that definition is exploratory (\S\ref{sec:conditions}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Two limitations deserve more than a clause. First, \textbf{every result is
conditioned on a single frozen baseline program}, one model
(\texttt{claude-opus-5}) and one repair step. The structural argument of
\S\ref{sec:blame} should carry to other programs of that shape, but no effect size
here is claimed to transfer to another program. A second baseline would test that
directly and is the most valuable single extension we can identify; it would also
tell us whether the refusal result of \S\ref{sec:refusal} is a property of this
program's structure or of the task and its success criterion.
Second, \textbf{the expressiveness audit is not exhaustive}. Our hand-written
repairs were a family of natural single- and double-edit candidates, not a search
of the program space, and the model found a repair that family missed, so
Table~\ref{tab:dsl} shows which natural edits fail, not which edits the DSL rules
out.

The remaining limitations are ordinary. $K{=}20$ with three replicates supports no
significance claim about improvement in repair success, and under the assumptions of Appendix~\ref{app:mde} only
large improvements in repair success were reliably detectable. We measure one repair step
per counterexample, with no regression feedback, so that every condition patches
the same frozen program; how effects compound over rounds is untested. F1 differs from the other conditions in length and information
content as well as in structure, so the edit-type contrast cannot be attributed
to any one of the three, and we ran no length-matched control
(\S\ref{sec:patchtype}). The specification change proposed in
\S\ref{sec:refusal} is untested. Seven of twenty certificates report no boundary, because
their sole boundary parameter was measured to be anti-correlated with the quantity
it names and was suppressed; those degrade to point counterexamples. Corpus
construction is amortized across all five conditions, so the cost asymmetry a
deployed closed loop would face is absent here. The simulator side reproduces,
apart from $\rhoc{4}$, but the model does not: it exposes no seed or temperature,
and its identifier names no snapshot.
Oncoming vehicles follow a scripted policy, holding their speed and, depending on
an aggressiveness parameter, braking when the ego nears the conflict zone, which
is simpler than naturalistic traffic; sensing is ground truth.

\paragraph{Broader impact.} This is a simulation study, and nothing in it is
evidence of real-world driving safety: contract satisfaction in CARLA, under
ground-truth sensing and simplified traffic, says little about a deployed
vehicle. The refusal result is itself a safety concern. A repair objective that
rewards only the absence of violations can yield systems that pass every
contract-based check while failing their task, and in real traffic a vehicle that
never proceeds creates hazards these contracts do not measure. The model's
patches also broke previously passing scenarios in three quarters of attempts,
so LLM-proposed repairs to safety-critical code need independent verification
before use.

\section{Conclusion}

The feedback conditions we compared differ sharply in what repair is attempted,
but because the raw trace differs in length and information content as well as
in structure, this is a difference between conditions, not a measured effect of
formal structure. Excluding refusals, we detected no condition improving repair
success, which at this sample size is not evidence that no such effect exists.

The more useful finding came from asking why so many resolved attempts never
entered the conflict zone. Contract satisfaction on a failing scenario is trivially achievable by
inaction, and neither completion scored on other scenarios nor a contract-based
regression suite reliably catches it. Applied in turn, contracts satisfied, manoeuvre
completed and regression suite intact select 18, then 3, then 1 of 300 attempts,
and a program meeting all three exists in this language for this corpus.

We conjecture that where a repair task scores only specification satisfaction
and the program representation admits inaction, the repairs that satisfy it will
tend to be refusals, though our data cannot separate this from the structure of
the one baseline we repaired. A second baseline, and a specification that rejects
vacuous satisfaction, would test it.

\section{Deviations from the internal study plan}
\label{app:deviations}

Our internal study plan, released with the code at
\url{https://github.com/mistryneel/counterexample-feedback/tree/v1.1-camera-ready/docs/study_plan},
pre-specified three contracts and four scenario parameters.
We ran five and six. The change was made before the baseline was frozen and for a
measured reason: on the reference program under three contracts, the violated
contract determined the blamed leaf exactly, so blame localization carried no
information beyond the contract label and the F3/F4 contrast would have been
vacuous by construction. We re-enabled the red-light and lead-distance contracts
to widen the range of failure modes. Of the four planned parameters, the
pedestrian-phase offset was dropped because no pedestrian was ever spawned, so it
had no effect; the signal offset and lead-vehicle gap were added because they make
the new contracts bind; and an aggressiveness parameter for oncoming vehicles was
added because without it only one parameter carried signal and every boundary
collapsed onto a single axis. We report this
because it is a deviation from the plan, and because it did not have the intended
effect: adding contracts does not by itself break the collapse, since each
contract may still map to a single leaf. What addressed it was the alternative
blame definition of \S\ref{sec:blame}, which is exploratory and labelled as such
throughout. The pre-specified field is \texttt{active\_node};
\texttt{authorising\_node} was defined after observing that the first was
constant, and no claim in this paper rests on F4b outperforming F3.

The pre-specified success criterion was $\rho \geq 0$ on the counterexample
scenario. We still report it, but after measuring that most of its successes
were refusals (\S\ref{sec:refusal}) we lead with the three criteria separately
rather than replacing it, so that the change is visible rather than silent.

\section{Edit taxonomy}
\label{app:taxonomy}

Patch type is a deterministic function of the baseline and patched sources
(\texttt{classify\_patch} in \texttt{src/ted.py}); no attempt was labelled by
hand. The skeleton of a parsed program, comments discarded, is the tree of node
names with every parameter removed and child order kept. If the skeletons
differ, the patch is a skeleton edit, which covers inserting, deleting or
reordering a node and replacing one node type with another. If they agree, it is
a hole fill when any parameter value differs after rounding to six decimals, and
no change otherwise; a program that fails to parse is unparseable. Only outputs
that pass the validator are classified.

The two categories, and the hypothesis that skeleton edits carry more regression
risk, were in the design specification before any repair attempt. The classifier
was unit tested and committed before the campaign, which ran from that commit;
apart from comments, it has not changed since. Because the label is computed, inter-rater
reliability does not arise; re-running the classifier on the logged programs
reproduces all 300 labels. Comparing edit-type rates across conditions was not
among the expectations we recorded in advance, which is why
\S\ref{sec:patchtype} reports it as an observed effect.

There were 170 hole fills, 130 skeleton edits, and no no-change or unparseable
outputs; Figure~\ref{fig:patchtype} shows the split by condition. Every skeleton edit increased the number of nodes, so no label rests on
a rename or a reordering alone. Of the hole fills, 140 changed at least one
action parameter, such as a target speed, and 30 changed only predicate
thresholds. Hole fills are not smaller edits: their median tree edit distance is
8 against 5 for skeleton edits, so the taxonomy measures the kind of edit, not
its size. Mean tree edit distance per patch was 5.93, 5.77, 6.95, 7.38 and 4.27
for F1 to F4b. Skeleton edits averaged 0.94 regression failures against 0.81 for
hole fills; patch type is an outcome confounded with condition, so we report this
descriptively and not as a test of the regression-risk hypothesis.

\section{Paired cluster bootstrap}
\label{app:bootstrap}

\paragraph{Unit.} The resampling unit is the counterexample certificate. There
are $K=20$, each attempted three times under each of the five conditions, so
every certificate carries 15 attempts and the design is balanced. Attempts on the
same certificate share its difficulty, and every condition is run on the same
certificates, so neither attempts nor conditions are independent; certificates
are.

\paragraph{Procedure.} Each of $10{,}000$ resamples draws 20 certificates
uniformly with replacement and keeps every attempt on each drawn certificate. For
each condition we compute the percentage of its attempts in the resample that
meet the outcome; the denominator is always 60. A difference between two
conditions is taken within the same resample, so both are always evaluated on the
same certificates. The $95\%$ interval runs from the $2.5$th to the $97.5$th
percentile of the $10{,}000$ differences (NumPy PCG64, seed 0, linear interpolation).
Because the design is balanced, this equals a percentile bootstrap of the mean of
the 20 certificate-level differences $100\,(s_{i,a}-s_{i,b})/3$, where $s_{i,a}$
counts attempts on certificate $i$ under condition $a$ that meet the outcome.
Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty} plots intervals from the same resamples.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} A \emph{structural edit} is a skeleton edit
(Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}). \emph{Success excluding refusals} requires
$\rho \ge 0$ on the counterexample and entry into the conflict zone; a refusal
stays in the denominator as a failure.

\paragraph{Comparisons.} We tabulate 17 paired differences: each condition against
F3 on four outcomes, and F1 against F2 on structural edits
(Table~\ref{tab:allci}). The intervals in the paper are unadjusted. Under a
Bonferroni adjustment over these 17, the structural-edit differences of F1 from F2
and from F3 still exclude zero; the two others that exclude zero unadjusted, F1
against F3 on pre-specified success and F4a against F3 on regressions, do not.
F1 also differs from F4a and F4b on structural edits, by $+73.3$
$[+53.3,+90.0]$ and $+51.7$ $[+36.7,+66.7]$ in the same resamples. Counting the
five further structural-edit pairs, we computed 22 distinct differences; under a
Bonferroni correction over all 22, exactly the four F1 structural-edit contrasts
exclude zero. F1 against F2, the contrast quoted in the
abstract, is the largest of the ten pairwise structural-edit differences, and
was singled out after seeing the data.

\paragraph{Limits.} With 20 clusters, percentile intervals can be somewhat too
narrow. Where a condition has no successes, its difference from F3 cannot be
positive, so an upper endpoint of exactly zero reflects absent events rather than
a precise estimate. For the joint criteria of Table~\ref{tab:criteria}, with zero
to two successes per condition, we report counts and no intervals.

\section{Detectable effect under stated assumptions}
\label{app:mde}

We estimated how often the decision rule of \S\ref{sec:null} would detect a true
difference $d$ in repair success between a condition and F3. Refusals count as
failures, so each rate is out of $60$ attempts. The rule is the paired cluster
bootstrap of Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}, with a detection declared when the
$95\%$ interval excludes zero. Because the design is balanced, the bootstrap
distribution depends only on the twenty per-certificate differences, so we
computed it exactly rather than from $10{,}000$ resamples; the two agreed on
$5{,}988$ of $6{,}000$ simulated datasets. A certificate's success probability is
Beta distributed with mean $p_0$ under F3 and $p_0+d$ under the other condition,
with intra-certificate correlation (ICC) of replicate outcomes in
$\{0, 0.1, 0.3\}$. Its difficulty is either shared across the two conditions,
taking the same quantile of each distribution, or independent, and replicates
are independent given the certificate. Table~\ref{tab:mde} gives the smallest
$d$ detected with probability $0.8$. Ignoring clustering, a two-proportion
calculation for $60$ attempts per arm gives $14.8$ points (arcsine
approximation) to $17.5$ points (Fisher's exact test) at $p_0=3.3\%$. At
$p_0=3.3\%$, a true improvement of $5$ points is detected with probability
$0.17$ to $0.23$ across these settings.

One property holds whatever the model. The interval cannot exclude zero unless
at least four of the twenty certificates favour the condition declared better,
so with F3 successful on at most three certificates under each repair-success
outcome (S, S excluding refusals, and S with C), no condition could have been
shown to do worse than F3 on repair success. Under the
model, a Bonferroni correction over the four comparisons against F3 raises each
entry by about $3$ to $5.5$ points, and over all 17 comparisons by about $6$ to
$10.5$. The entries move with the assumptions, most of all with how strongly
difficulty is shared, and are properties of the design rather than a measured
detection limit. The data cannot estimate either assumption, but the five successes
excluding refusals fell one per cell and on five different certificates, a
pattern less likely under strongly shared difficulty than under weak clustering,
so the lowest entries are the optimistic ones.
